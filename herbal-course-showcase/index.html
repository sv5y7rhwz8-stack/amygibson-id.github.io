    Herbal Identification & First Aid · Course Overview

    Welcome to Herbal Identification & First Aid

    Long before modern pharmacology, communities relied on careful, hard-won plant knowledge passed down through generations — knowing exactly which plant to reach for, and just as importantly, which to leave alone. This course introduces you to that tradition through a practical, safety-first lens: learning to identify common medicinal herbs, understand how they've traditionally been used, and prepare simple herbal remedies responsibly.

    By the End of This Course, You Will Be Able To:

    1

    Explain the historical significance of medicinal plants and the importance of safe herbal identification and use.

    2

    Identify common medicinal herbs by their distinguishing characteristics.

    3

    Apply knowledge of medicinal herbs to select appropriate herbs for common traditional first aid situations.

    4

    Prepare a simple immune-boosting syrup with commonly used herbs while demonstrating responsible stewardship of medicinal plants.

    🌿 Backyard Botanist

    An Identification & Community Discussion

    This week, you'll step outside your own door and put your identification skills to work — then bring what you find back to the group. Herbalists have always learned this way: not in isolation, but by comparing notes, questioning each other, and building shared knowledge over time. This activity is your chance to do the same with your classmates.

    Part 1: Find & Post

    Find one plant near you this week that you cannot confidently identify — in your yard, on a walk, along a fence line, wherever you happen to be. Photograph it (leaves, flowers or seed heads if present, and the overall growth habit), then post to the discussion forum with:

    • Your best guess at an ID, and the features that led you there
    • Where you found it and what kind of habitat it's growing in (sun/shade, wet/dry, disturbed soil, garden bed, etc.)
    • One thing you're genuinely unsure about

    Part 2: Respond & Confirm

    Reply to at least two classmates' posts. You might:

    • Confirm their ID and explain what supports it
    • Offer an alternate ID with your reasoning
    • Name a common look-alike they should watch for in that region

    Since our class spans different climates and regions, don't be surprised if a plant that's a total mystery to one person is old news to another — that's exactly the point.

    Part 3: Close the Loop (optional)

    Once your plant's ID feels reasonably confirmed by the group, come back and add a short follow-up: does it have any traditional or herbal use where you live? This turns your identification into the start of your own personal apothecary notes.

    Look closely. Compare notes. Grow your knowledge together.

    Interactive: browse each specimen, compare traits side by side, then test your eye.

    Herbal Identification & First Aid · Formative Check

    Field Notes Check-In: Identifying Elderberry, Dandelion & Echinacea

    1Directions

    Using what you learned in the Field Guide to Distinguishing Traits, read the three plant descriptions below. For each one, name the plant it describes — elderberry, dandelion, or echinacea — and point to at least two specific characteristics from the description (leaf shape, flower structure, habitat, or growth pattern) that led you to your answer.

    This is a formative check, not a graded practical. The point is to see where your eye for identification stands right now, so use your notes freely and don't stress over getting every detail perfect.

    • Specimen A — A tangle of small white flowers clusters in a flat crown atop a woody, multi-stemmed shrub growing along a creek bank.
    • Specimen B — Jagged-edged leaves lie flat in a rosette with no true stem, and a bright yellow bloom rises on a hollow stalk at the edge of a lawn.
    • Specimen C — Rough, lance-shaped leaves alternate up a sturdy stem, topped by drooping rose-purple petals around a spiky, raised center, growing in a dry open field.
    Response format example (not one of the specimens above) Specimen D is calendula — I identified it from its bright orange, daisy-like flower head with many narrow petals and its low, bushy growth habit in a sunny garden bed.

    2Rubric

    Criterion Points
    Correct plant identified (1 pt each × 3 specimens) 3
    At least two accurate, specific traits cited per specimen (1 pt each × 3) 3
    Total 6
    Credit is given for sound reasoning even if a final answer is close but not exact — this check is about whether you're reading traits correctly, not about a perfect score. You'll get a short written or video comment back on your submission rather than just a number.

    3Due Date: July 29th

    Submit your answers directly in the text-entry box for this assignment by [Wednesday, July 29th], before our next module on herbal safety.

    Since this is a formative check meant to help you self-assess, late submissions are accepted up to 24 hours after the deadline with no penalty — just reach out if you need more time than that.

    Behind the build

    Design decisions

    A few of the choices that shaped how this module teaches.

    Safety before use

    Identification comes before any remedy. The module opens with why misidentification matters, and every resource repeats the same message: know the plant first, then consult a professional for anything serious.

    Practice without pressure

    The Field Note Check-In is a low-stakes formative check. It gives credit for sound reasoning, takes late work without penalty, and returns written or video feedback instead of just a score.

    Learning from each other

    Backyard Botanist sends learners outside to photograph a plant they can't identify, then has classmates confirm it, offer another ID, or name look-alikes. Because learners live in different regions, everyone brings something new.

    One cohesive look

    A shared palette of forest green, parchment and amber, with Playfair Display and EB Garamond, ties every page, the microlecture and the interactives together, so the module reads like one field journal.
